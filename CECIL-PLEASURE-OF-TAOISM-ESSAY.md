# The Pleasure of Taoism: Questions from Wes Cecil's Lecture, Answered and Argued With

*A study essay on "The Pleasure of Taoism," a lecture by Wes Cecil*

Most lectures about Taoism are arguments about Taoism, and Wes Cecil begins his by refusing to make one. The tradition he is describing, he says, does not offer an explanation of life, or a construct of life that you can intellectualize. It offers a sensibility that you can experience. That single distinction — between a doctrine you hold and a way of tasting the world — organizes everything that follows, and it generates, one after another, a series of questions that sound simple until you try to live inside them. This essay takes Cecil's questions in the order his lecture raises them. For each one it gives the Taoist answer as Cecil presents it, faithfully and with his hedges intact; then it argues back as strongly and honestly as it can; then it says where the exchange leaves a reader who is trying to decide whether any of this is true.

## What does tranquility feel like?

The lecture's first and governing question is not what tranquility means but what it is like. Cecil's answer is that tranquility is a condition, not a position: the state of a pool when the dirt has settled and the water has gone still. You do not argue yourself into it. What fills you when you arrive there, he says, is pleasure — joy, a sense of well-being, an appreciation of what is coming and what is going. Crucially, this pleasure is not grasped, sought, achieved, deserved, or earned. It comes to you and it goes away, like everything else in a process philosophy, and every part of the process can be great: the morning, the noon, and the night. Taoist texts sometimes ask which is better, the morning, the noon, or the night, and Cecil calls it a silly question — they are different, and underneath the difference each can carry the same sense of joy.

The counter-argument is worth stating at full strength, because a great deal of human life is organized against it. A feeling, the objection runs, is not a philosophy; a sensibility that cannot be stated cannot be taught, tested, or corrected, and a pleasure that arrives unbidden cannot be a program for anyone whose pool is muddy for reasons that are not their fault — grief, illness, poverty, a mind that will not settle. There is also a quiet circularity in defining the goal as a feeling and then pointing to the feeling as evidence the goal was reached. And yet the exchange leaves Cecil's opening mostly standing, because he never claimed tranquility was an argument in the first place. The objection defeats a doctrine he declined to offer. What he offered was a description — when the water clears, this is what people report — and descriptions are tested not in debate but in the tasting, which is precisely where the lecture goes next.

## The vinegar: correct it, escape it, or smile?

The lecture's centerpiece is the old painting of the Three Vinegar Tasters, a theme Cecil dates, with a hedge he flags himself, to the Song Dynasty and its centuries of repainting. Three sages sample wine that has turned to vinegar, and their faces give three philosophies. Confucius finds it sour: life is sour, and needs changing, affecting, controlling. The Buddha finds it bitter: bitterness is the nature of life, and the theoretical goal is to let go of it and escape it. The Taoist smiles. Cecil is scrupulous here, and the scruple matters: there are two readings of the smile, and he puts both on the table. In the earlier and, he thinks, better reading, the Taoist smiles because vinegar tastes the way vinegar is supposed to taste. In the later reading, he smiles because it is sweet, and life is sweet. Cecil also notes, with the same honesty, that an older layer of the tradition had all three sages react alike — a reminder that even the parable has been argued over by the schools that own it.

Take the smile's first reading, since it is the stronger one. Its claim is that bitterness and sourness are not properties of the vinegar but of the expectation: you wanted wine. If you have tranquility, what comes is what comes. Vinegar is good on a salad; aged vinegar is a delicacy people pay for. The disappointment was manufactured by the label in your head.

Now the counter-arguments, and there are three, one for each sage. The Confucian answers first: some vinegar really is spoilage. Wine turns because someone was careless, and the world is full of turned wine that did not have to turn — corrupt courts, broken promises, children unfed. To smile at all of it is to confuse acceptance with surrender, and the Confucian project of correcting, channeling, and ordering life has, at its best, drained more real bitterness out of the world than any smile. The Buddhist answers second: smiling is still a reaction of the palate, and a philosophy that ends in enjoying the taste has not escaped the tongue. Pleasure at vinegar is attachment wearing a pleasant face; the bitterness to be released includes the need for life to taste good. And the commonsense objection answers third: the parable rigs its own test. Vinegar is a trivial disappointment. Nobody's sage is asked to smile at the genuinely intolerable, and a wisdom that works at the salad bar may have nothing to say at the graveside. Where the exchange leaves us is narrower than the painting but sturdier: Cecil's Taoist is not making a claim about all suffering. He is making a claim about expectation — that a large share of ordinary dissatisfaction is the gap between what arrived and what we demanded — and that claim survives both sages' rebuttals, because neither Confucius nor the Buddha denied that the gap exists. They only disagreed about what to do with it.

## Do the five colors blind us?

Cecil grounds the lecture's middle in chapter 12 of the Tao Te Ching, quoting Red Pine's version: "The five colors make our eyes blind, the five tones make our ears deaf, the five flavors make our mouths numb, riding and hunting make our minds wild, hard to get goods make us break laws. Thus the sage puts the stomach ahead of the eyes." His gloss is experiential: if you are busy sorting the world into the colors you want, you cannot see. His two witnesses are artists. Debussy, at the great academy, played chords his teachers could not hear — not because the teachers were unmusical, but because the rules of composition they had learned made them deaf, and they threatened to expel him for sounds that are now counted among the most beautiful ever made. Van Gogh is the second witness: nobody was trained to paint as he painted, and rule-bound eyes read his seeing as error. The question the chapter presses is whether our categories — the five colors in the head — are instruments of perception or replacements for it.

The counter-argument here is strong and should not be softened. Categories are also how anyone learns to hear at all. Debussy could break the academy's rules because the academy had first given him a musical language exact enough to break; his chords are not the sounds of a man who never studied, and the teachers' deafness was purchased by the same training that produced the student who surpassed them. Medicine doses by category, engineering builds by it, and a cook who refused the five flavors on principle would be a worse cook, not a sage. The distinction Cecil needs — and, to be fair, the one his phrasing supports — is between categories held lightly as tools and categories held as verdicts: the teachers' failure was not that they had rules but that they treated a rule's violation as proof of worthlessness before they had listened. On that reading the chapter is not an attack on knowledge. It is a warning about the order of operations: taste first, then classify, and never let the classification do the tasting for you.

## Are we tasting the thing, or its marketing?

From the five flavors Cecil turns to the table. His friend, a wine writer of decades' standing for magazines and newspapers, points out that the champagnes most people can name are the ones that advertise — so when you drink them you are not tasting champagne; you are drinking marketing. You drink with your eyes: the recognized label tells you it is good, you pay more because you know it is good, and none of this has anything to do with the flavor profile you actually want or how your stomach responds. The sage's alternative is to eat with the stomach — to ask of a thing whether it appeals to you, nourishes you, makes you feel good — rather than with the eyes, which ask whether it is expensive, rare, or correctly labeled. Cecil offers a deliberately homely proof from television advertising: a heartburn drug, sold over the counter, whose entire promise is that you may go on eating the foods that make you ill. The Taoist response, he says, is almost embarrassingly simple. If it makes you feel ill, listen. Don't do things that make you ill.

The counter-argument concedes the diagnosis and questions the cure. Labels and reputations are not pure fraud; they are information, compressed experience, and in a world of ten thousand bottles no stomach has time to taste them all — the wine writer's own expertise, after all, reaches Cecil secondhand, as a trusted label of another kind. Nor is the stomach an incorruptible judge: appetites are trained, sugars and salts are engineered to please palates they then ruin, and the heartburn patient's stomach "says yes" in the moment as clearly as it says no at midnight. Cecil's answer to that is embedded in his own example, though: the stomach he means is not the craving of the moment but the whole body's settled response, pleasure and consequence together. Measured that way, the marketing objection and the Taoist prescription turn out to agree on the essential point — both distrust the first signal, whether it comes from a label or a craving, and both ask what the thing does to you when the whole of you has had its say.

## What are clothes for?

The five colors reappear, in the lecture, as fashion. Clothes covered in brands and labels, Cecil argues, have a purpose that is not the clothes: they announce. A band shirt tells strangers what music you like; a logo tells them what you can spend or which group you belong to — and Taoism, which does not want to tell people things, regards the billboard garment as a small assault on everyone who has to read it. Against this he sets a different set of signals entirely: beauty, elegance, comfort, appropriateness, humility, tranquility. Dress so that you feel pleased when you dress, and pleased in the world, and so that other people feel good around you — be kind, be kind, be kind, and think of other people's experience, because if we stir up their pond and generate mud, we will likely suffer in the same water. The sage of the earlier readings is invisible, not from shame but from courtesy: dressing that does not disturb the water. And Cecil supplies the cautionary tale whole. A factory worker of his acquaintance wore coveralls and a hard hat on the job, but wanted to be thought a professional, so he drove to work each morning in a suit, carrying a briefcase, and changed into his uniform at the plant. His coworkers, who were not stupid, mocked him. The costume bought him insecurity — the precise opposite of its price — because it was chosen entirely by his eyes, for other people's eyes, and his own skin was never consulted.

The counter-argument has to defend the billboard a little, because signaling is not vanity alone. Human beings read dress the way they read faces, and a uniform honestly worn — the actual coveralls, the nurse's scrubs, the mourner's black — is communication in the service of the work, not instead of it. Even the band shirt can be a genuine pleasure and an invitation to kinship rather than a boast; Cecil himself allows that any choice of clothing sends signals unavoidably, so the question cannot be whether to signal but which signals and at whose expense. The factory worker, judged on Cecil's own terms, is thus a subtler case than a fool in a costume: his error was not dressing well but dressing as someone he was not, for an audience whose good opinion the disguise could never safely buy. Where the exchange leaves us is close to Cecil's own conclusion. He does not issue a dress code — a path that is a path is no path — but a direction of questioning: what fabrics feel good on you, what colors appeal to you, what makes you comfortable and at ease, asked from the stomach and not from the eyes. A person who dresses by those answers will still communicate. What they communicate will simply be true.

## What about the things I am supposed to like?

One of the lecture's most humane questions arrives quietly: what happens when you taste the thing you were told to like, and you don't like it? Cecil's observation is that most people conclude something is wrong with them, and set about changing themselves until they like what they are supposed to like and dislike what they are not. Our culture even has a name for the reverse case — the guilty pleasure, a thing you enjoy that you are not supposed to — and Cecil finds the concept itself bizarre. Watch with your stomach. His witness is himself: he does not like crowds or noisy environments, and in high school and college he went to the parties he was invited to, liking the people and believing he should like the going, and was miserable every time — until he learned simply to say that he will not be at noisy crowded things, a sentence he has now been saying, peaceably, for the rest of his life. And he is careful to universalize it in both directions: Peter Gabriel sings of loving the rush hour precisely for the rush, the moving of the people, and that is not Cecil, but it may truly be Gabriel, and both are fine. There is no definitive rule. There is only listening to yourself and reporting honestly what you hear.

The counter-argument notes, first, that taste is partly trainable and sometimes worth training — the acquired tastes for bitter greens, difficult music, and other people's company in crowds are real goods that pure stomach-listening, applied too early, would have refused; the man who never goes to the party may be protecting a sensitivity or may be shrinking a life, and the stomach alone cannot always tell him which. Second, other people have stakes in our tastes: the friend whose invitation is always refused eventually stops inviting, and a sensibility that never bends can stir ponds of its own. But these are qualifications rather than defeats, because Cecil's rule contains its own limit — the same lecture that frees you from the party obliges you to kindness and to the neighbors' pond. What his argument abolishes is not effort or courtesy but the guilt: the idea that disliking what you are told to like is a defect of character. A taste reported honestly, and declined kindly, is the whole of his demand, and it is hard to see who is harmed by it.

## Is pleasure really the measure?

Everything in the lecture builds toward its boldest claim, the one Cecil himself flags as a reversal of Western common sense. Pleasure is not a sideline or a reward; it is the metric. If you are feeling yourself, feeling your own internal sensibility, then you know you are doing it correctly — not because you followed a set of rules, but because you are experiencing the world in a particular way, and for Taoism that experience is the truth. Our culture, he observes, assigns tranquility to the monastery and pleasure to Las Vegas, two spiky exceptions that have little to do with an ordinary life; Taoism reverses the arrangement and puts pleasure in the daily acts — eating, dressing — where most of a life actually happens.

This claim needs the strongest counter-arguments of the essay, and there are three. The first is that pleasure lies: some pleasures are poisons on a delay, and addiction is precisely the condition of a stomach that reports "correctly" for a thing that is destroying it; a metric that cannot distinguish nourishment from craving will bless the road to ruin. The second is adaptation: pleasure habituates, the champagne that delighted becomes the champagne that is merely expected, and a life steered by the metric can become a treadmill of escalation — the very "hard to get goods" the Tao Te Ching warns make us break laws. The third is conflict: pleasures collide. My loud music is your stirred pond, and when metrics disagree there must be an arbiter above pleasure, which is what rules and hierarchies were invented to be. What can be said for Cecil's side of the exchange is that his metric is more specific than its slogan. The pleasure he means is the pleasure *of tranquility* — settled, unpursued, arriving when the water clears — and poison-pleasures, treadmill pleasures, and pond-stirring pleasures all fail that description on inspection: they agitate rather than settle, they must be grasped rather than received, and they muddy the neighbors' water, which his own ethic forbids. That does not fully answer the addiction objection — the addict's calm is a counterfeit that can mimic the real one from the inside — but it does mean the metric is a compound one: pleasure, yes, and also stillness, and also kindness, each checking the others. Read that way, the claim is less a license than a discipline, and a demanding one.

## Conclusion: vinegar, tasted honestly

Near the lecture's end, Cecil gathers his counsel into a single sentence about the vat: don't be upset because it is not something else, and don't try to see if it is the best vinegar that ever was. It is the whole teaching in miniature. The resentment of what arrived, because it is not what was ordered, and the ranking of what arrived, because we must know where it stands — these are the two movements of the unquiet mind, and Taoism declines them both. What it puts in their place is almost disappointingly ungrand: a stomach instead of eyes, a settled pool instead of a stirred one, daily acts done in a way that gives pleasure because those acts are most of a life. The counter-arguments gathered here are real, and Cecil's lecture is better for surviving the honest ones and bending around the rest: rules also teach, categories also heal, some vinegar is spoilage and should be poured out by somebody's Confucian hand. But the center holds. Before the correction and before the ranking, there is the tasting, and a tradition that has lasted this long by insisting we begin there — that existence, attended to without demand, is good — has earned the smile in the painting. The Taoist is not the sage who knows the most about the vinegar. He is the one who has stopped arguing with it long enough to find out what it actually tastes like.
